# Cover letter elements and declarations

## 1. What editors look for

| Question | Where the letter answers it |
|---|---|
| What is this paper? | Opening: title, article type, journal |
| What does it find? | Second paragraph: question, approach, main result with a number |
| Why does it matter? | Third paragraph: the advance and who will use it |
| Why this journal? | Third paragraph: scope and readership, with a recent related article in the journal |
| Is it eligible? | Declarations |

Editors often decide whether to send a paper to review in minutes. The letter's job is to make that decision easy.

## 2. Declarations commonly required

| Declaration | Standard wording (to be confirmed by the authors) |
|---|---|
| Originality and exclusivity | "This manuscript is original, has not been published, and is not under consideration elsewhere." |
| Author approval | "All authors have read and approved the manuscript and agree to its submission." |
| Conflicts of interest | "The authors declare no competing interests." or the specific interests |
| Funding | "This work was supported by <funder> (grant <number>)." |
| Ethics approval | "The study was approved by <committee> (approval <number>); participants gave informed consent." |
| Data availability | "Data are available at <repository/DOI>" or the restriction and access route |
| Preprint | "A preprint is available at <server and DOI>." |
| Prior presentation | "Preliminary results were presented at <conference, year>." |
| Related submissions | Disclosure of overlapping papers under review |
| AI use | Statement per the journal's policy where required |

Every declaration is a statement of fact made by the authors. Use placeholders until they confirm.

## 3. Significance sentence patterns

- "This is the first <design> to show <finding> in <population>, which <consequence>." (only with a search that supports "first")
- "These results resolve <contradiction> by showing that <...>."
- "The method reduces <cost> by <x>, making <application> practical for <users>."
- "The dataset fills a gap: existing resources cover <...> but not <...>."

## 4. Fit to the journal

Name the journal's scope phrase that applies and, where possible, one or two articles the journal published recently that this paper builds on or answers. Verify those articles exist and describe them accurately.

## 5. Suggested and opposed reviewers

| Rule | |
|---|---|
| Independent | No co-authorship in the last 3 to 5 years, no shared institution, no close collaboration |
| Qualified | Recent publications on the topic |
| Verifiable | Institutional email from an official page |
| Diverse | Different institutions and countries |
| Opposed reviewers | Name only with a brief, neutral reason (for example a direct competitor); editors may disregard |

Never suggest friends, collaborators, or invented contacts. Fabricated reviewer details have led to mass retractions.

## 6. Highlights and significance statements

| Item | Typical rules |
|---|---|
| Elsevier highlights | 3 to 5 bullets; a character limit per bullet (check the guide for authors); each a finding or contribution |
| Significance statement (some journals, for example PNAS) | 120 words or fewer for a general reader |
| Graphical abstract | Single image summarising the main finding |
| Plain-language summary | Non-specialist audience; journal sets the length |

## 7. Revision cover letters

Reference the manuscript number; thank the editor and reviewers in one sentence; list the main changes in three to five bullets; point to the attached response; restate any changed declarations.

## 8. Transfer or resubmission after rejection

Some journals ask whether the paper was previously rejected. Answer honestly, summarise how it was improved, and offer the previous reviews if the journal's transfer system supports it.

## 9. Tone and length

One page. Plain, specific, and confident without superlatives. No flattery of the journal. Address the handling editor by name when known.
