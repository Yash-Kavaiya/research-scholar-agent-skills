# Introduction moves, phrasing, and calibration

## 1. The CARS model (Swales)

| Move | Step | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Establish a territory | Claim centrality | Shows the area matters |
| | Review items of previous research | Summarises what is known |
| 2 Establish a niche | Counter-claim, indicate a gap, raise a question, or continue a tradition | Shows what is missing |
| 3 Occupy the niche | State the purpose or describe the present work | Says what this paper does |
| | Announce principal findings | Previews the results |
| | State the value | Says why the findings matter |
| | Outline the structure | Maps the paper (optional) |

## 2. Paragraph plan for a research paper

| Paragraph | Content | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Context specific to the problem; why it matters, with one or two citations | 3–5 sentences |
| 2 | The problem and how it is currently approached | 3–6 sentences |
| 3 | The gap: what current approaches cannot do, with evidence | 3–5 sentences |
| 4 | This paper's approach and why it should work | 3–5 sentences |
| 5 | Results preview with numbers, then the contributions list | 3–6 sentences plus bullets |
| 6 | Paper outline (only if the venue expects it) | 1–2 sentences |

Journals and theses allow longer introductions; the order holds.

## 3. Gap phrasing

| Gap type | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Missing evidence | "Whether <X> holds for <Y> has not been tested; existing studies examine only <Z> (A; B)." |
| Limitation of methods | "Current methods require <X>, which is unavailable in <setting> (A; B)." |
| Conflict | "Studies disagree on <X>: A report <...>, whereas B find <...>." |
| Scale or setting | "Prior evaluations use <small or narrow setting>; behaviour at <scale or setting> is unknown." |
| Explanation | "<Phenomenon> is well documented, but why it occurs is not understood." |

Support every gap with citations, and keep it narrow enough that the paper closes it.

## 4. Contribution list

- Two to four items.
- Each is a deliverable (method, finding, resource, analysis), not an activity.
- Each points to the section with the evidence.
- The list matches the abstract and the conclusion.

| Activity (weak) | Deliverable (strong) |
|---|---|
| We conduct extensive experiments. | We show that X holds across five datasets and two model families (Section 5). |
| We study the problem of X. | We formalise X as Y and give the first algorithm with guarantee Z (Section 3). |
| We propose a novel framework. | We introduce M, which removes the need for paired data (Section 3). |

## 5. Calibrating claims

| Evidence | Verb |
|---|---|
| Proof | prove, establish |
| Controlled experiment, replicated | show, demonstrate |
| Experiment with limits | find, provide evidence that |
| Correlational or partial | suggest, indicate, is associated with |
| Position or argument | argue, propose |

"First" requires a qualifier ("to our knowledge") and a search that supports it. "Novel" adds little; describe what is new instead.

## 6. Openings to avoid and alternatives

| Avoid | Instead |
|---|---|
| "In recent years, X has attracted increasing attention." | Start with the specific problem and a concrete fact. |
| "X is a hot topic." | State why X matters with evidence. |
| A history of the field from its origins | One sentence of context, then the problem. |
| A dictionary definition | Define the term precisely where it is needed. |

## 7. Figure 1 reference

Refer to Figure 1 by the second or third paragraph so readers who skim the figures can connect them to the argument.

## 8. Consistency checks

| Check | Against |
|---|---|
| Gap statement | Related work section |
| Contributions | Abstract and conclusion |
| Numbers in the results preview | Results tables |
| Terms and names | Method section |

## 9. Thesis introductions

Add: background at greater depth, the problem statement, aims and research questions, scope and delimitations, significance, a summary of contributions by chapter, and the thesis outline.
