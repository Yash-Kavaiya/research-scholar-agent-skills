# Finding and approaching supervisors

## 1. Sources for identifying active researchers

| Source | What it gives |
|---|---|
| Recent papers on the topic (last 3–4 years) | Senior or corresponding authors who lead groups |
| Programme committees and editorial boards of the topic's venues | Recognised researchers in the area |
| Department faculty pages | Current faculty and their stated interests |
| Funded project databases (national funders) | Who has current funding on the topic |
| Workshops and tutorials on the topic | Organisers active in the area |
| Google Scholar, ORCID, DBLP profiles | Publication lists and co-author networks |
| Lab websites | Members, openings, how to apply |

## 2. Verification checklist per person

| Item | Where |
|---|---|
| Current position and institution | Official faculty or lab page |
| Can supervise at the required level | Department rules; the person's page |
| Taking students for the intended intake | Lab page "openings", "prospective students" |
| Recent focus | Publications from the last 2–3 years |
| Instructions for prospective students | Lab or personal page |
| Funding available | Advertised positions; programme funding rules |
| Contact address | Official page only |

## 3. Judging fit

| Factor | Signal |
|---|---|
| Topical overlap | Two or more recent papers close to your interest |
| Methods | Uses the methods you know or want to learn |
| Group size | Large groups: more peers, less individual time; small groups: the reverse |
| Student outcomes | Recent graduates' destinations; time to degree |
| Supervision style | Ask current students where possible |
| Practical | Location, funding, language, visa, start date |

## 4. Reading a candidate supervisor's work

Read the abstract and introduction of two or three recent papers and the full text of one. Note one specific idea you could build on or a question it raises. That note becomes the email's second paragraph.

## 5. Email structure

| Part | Content | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Prospective <degree> student (<intake>): <specific topic> | One line |
| Opening | Who you are, your current position, the purpose | Two sentences |
| Connection | A specific, accurate link to their recent paper and to your experience | Three or four sentences |
| Ask | Are they taking students for <intake>? Would they be open to a short call? | One or two sentences |
| Close | Attachments (CV, transcript if customary), thanks, name | Two lines |

Total 120 to 180 words, plain text.

## 6. Example (illustrative)

> Subject: Prospective PhD student (Fall 2027): robust speech recognition for low-resource languages
>
> Dear Professor <Surname>,
>
> I am a final-year MTech student at <institution>, working on speech recognition for Gujarati. I am applying to <programme> for Fall 2027.
>
> Your 2025 paper on <specific topic> showed that <specific finding>. In my thesis I found a related effect: <brief result>. I would like to study whether <question connecting the two>.
>
> Are you planning to take PhD students for Fall 2027? If so, I would be grateful for a brief conversation. My CV is attached.
>
> Thank you for your time.
> <Name>

(Names and findings are placeholders to be replaced with verified details.)

## 7. Etiquette

- Send individually; never bulk email with visible lists.
- Follow stated instructions on the person's page.
- One follow-up after 10 to 14 days; then stop.
- Apply through the official process regardless of the reply.
- Use a professional address.

## 8. Tracking

Keep a table with name, institution, date contacted, response, next step, and deadlines (research-progress-tracker can hold it).
