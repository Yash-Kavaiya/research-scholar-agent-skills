# Synthesis patterns for literature reviews

## 1. Structures

| Structure | Organise by | Use when | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thematic | Concepts, variables, or sub-questions | The default; several strands bear on the question | Themes overlap; define each |
| Methodological | How the topic has been studied | The methods are the debate, or findings depend on method | Becomes a methods catalogue |
| Chronological | Periods or phases | The development of ideas is the argument | Turns into a list by date |
| Theoretical | Frameworks or schools | Competing explanations exist | Loses the empirical findings |
| Funnel | Broad context to the specific gap | Short reviews and introductions | Too much general background |

Most thesis chapters combine: thematic sections, with a short chronological or theoretical opening.

## 2. From matrix to outline

1. Each theme column of the synthesis matrix becomes a section.
2. Read down a column and write its claim: what the studies together show, where they disagree, what is missing.
3. Order sections so each prepares the next and the last one arrives at the gap.
4. Write section headings as claims where the style allows, or keep a claim sentence under each topic heading.

## 3. Paragraph patterns

**Convergence.** Several studies agree.
> Topic claim. Evidence from A, B, and C in one sentence, noting their different settings. What this consistency establishes. One limitation they share.

**Divergence.** Studies disagree.
> Topic claim that findings are mixed. One side with sources. The other side with sources. The difference that may explain it (sample, measure, context, period). What remains unresolved.

**Development.** Understanding changed over time.
> Earlier position with sources. What challenged it. Current position. What the shift implies for the present question.

**Method critique.** A shared weakness.
> What most studies did. Why that limits the conclusions. The few that did otherwise and what they found.

**Gap.** Nothing, or too little, exists.
> What is established nearby. What has not been examined, with the search that supports saying so. Why it matters.

## 4. Verbs and connectors that do analytic work

| Purpose | Words |
|---|---|
| Agreement | similarly, consistent with, corroborates, in line with |
| Contrast | however, in contrast, whereas, conversely, challenges |
| Extension | builds on, extends, refines |
| Limitation | is limited by, does not address, leaves open |
| Evidence strength | demonstrates (strong), indicates, suggests (weaker), reports (neutral), claims or argues (position) |

Choose the reporting verb by the strength of the source's evidence. "Proves" belongs to mathematics.

## 5. Summary versus synthesis

| Summary (avoid) | Synthesis (aim for) |
|---|---|
| "Smith (2020) studied 200 teachers and found burnout was high. Jones (2021) surveyed nurses and found burnout was linked to workload." | "Workload is the most consistent predictor of burnout across professions (Smith, 2020; Jones, 2021), although both studies rely on self-report at a single time point." |
| One source per paragraph | Several sources per point |
| Author as the grammatical subject | The idea as the subject; authors in parentheses or as evidence |

## 6. Critical evaluation moves

For each cluster of studies ask and, where it matters, write:

- How strong is the design for the claim being made?
- Are the samples and settings comparable to the user's context?
- Do the studies share a measurement weakness?
- Who funded or conducted them, and does that matter?
- Has the finding been replicated?

## 7. Opening and closing

**Introduction to the review:** scope and boundaries; how sources were found and selected (databases, years, criteria), briefly; how the review is organised.

**Closing section:** what is established; what is contested; what is unknown; the specific gap; how the present study addresses it. The reader should finish knowing why the study is needed.

## 8. Citation practice

- Cite the primary study for a finding, not a paper that cites it.
- Use page numbers for quotations, and quote rarely.
- Integrate citations in one of two ways and vary them: information-prominent "(Author, Year)" or author-prominent "Author (Year) argues".
- Group citations supporting the same point in one parenthesis, ordered as the style requires.
- Balance seminal work with the last two or three years.

## 9. Tense

| Use | Tense |
|---|---|
| What a specific study did or found | Past: "Lee (2022) found" |
| Established knowledge and the state of the literature | Present: "Research shows", "little is known" |
| A continuing line of work | Present perfect: "Studies have examined" |

## 10. Revision checklist

- Each paragraph starts with a point about the literature.
- No paragraph rests on a single source unless that source is the point.
- Every disagreement is named and, where possible, explained.
- Every section ends by linking to the next or to the gap.
- Every claim has a supplied or retrieved source; unsupported ones are marked.
- The final section states the gap in terms that match the research question.
